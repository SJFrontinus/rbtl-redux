Atherogenicity of Lipoprotein(a) Versus LDL: A Comprehensive Analysis
Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) both contribute to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, yet Lp(a) is substantially more atherogenic on a per-particle basis. This distinction has critical implications for cardiovascular risk assessment and clinical management. Recent Mendelian randomization analyses and mechanistic studies reveal fundamental differences in how these lipoproteins drive vascular pathology.

Superior Per-Particle Atherogenicity of Lipoprotein(a)
The most striking difference between Lp(a) and LDL is their relative atherogenic potency when compared particle-for-particle. Since both lipoproteins contain exactly one apoB molecule per particle, researchers can use apoB as a direct quantitative comparator. Mendelian randomization studies leveraging genome-wide association data from over 500,000 participants in the UK Biobank demonstrate that Lp(a) is approximately 6- to 7-fold more atherogenic than LDL on an equimolar basis. This estimate is derived from genetic variants uniquely affecting Lp(a) mass concentration versus those affecting LDL concentration. The hazard ratio for coronary heart disease per 50 nmol/L apoB was 1.28 (95% CI: 1.24-1.33) for Lp(a)-apoB compared to just 1.04 (95% CI: 1.03-1.05) for LDL-apoB. In polygenic score analyses, the coronary heart disease risk per 50 nmol/L of apoB was 1.47 for the Lp(a) cluster versus 1.04 for the LDL cluster.

This heightened per-particle risk translates to meaningful clinical implications. To achieve the same cardiovascular risk reduction through Lp(a) lowering as achieved through a 1.0 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol, approximately 101.5 mg/dL (220 nmol/L) of Lp(a) would need to be lowered—a substantial difference. However, since circulating Lp(a) particle concentrations are typically far lower than LDL particle concentrations, even in individuals with markedly elevated Lp(a), the absolute contribution to total apoB-containing particles remains limited. On a population level, despite Lp(a)'s heightened per-particle atherogenicity, LDL particles—which vastly outnumber Lp(a) particles—account for greater absolute cardiovascular burden in most individuals.

Distinct Mechanistic Pathways of Atherogenicity
The atherogenic mechanisms of Lp(a) diverge substantially from those of LDL, explaining its heightened per-particle risk. While LDL promotes atherosclerosis primarily through passive cholesterol accumulation and oxidative modification in the arterial wall, Lp(a) engages multiple overlapping pathways that amplify vascular damage.

Oxidized Phospholipid Content and Inflammation. Lp(a) is the predominant carrier of oxidized phospholipids (OxPL) in human plasma. These OxPL reside primarily on the apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] component of Lp(a) particles. In vivo imaging studies of patients with elevated Lp(a) demonstrate markedly increased arterial wall inflammation measured by 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography, with intensity correlating proportionately to Lp(a) levels. Human monocytes isolated from individuals with elevated Lp(a) (median 108 mg/dL versus 7 mg/dL in controls) display a primed, long-lasting inflammatory state characterized by enhanced capacity to transmigrate the endothelium and produce proinflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1β, interleukin-6) upon stimulation. Critically, this enhanced monocyte inflammatory response is directly mediated by OxPL on Lp(a), as removal or inactivation of OxPL markedly attenuates the proinflammatory effect in healthy monocytes exposed to Lp(a) in vitro. These oxidized phospholipids likely trigger inflammation through pattern recognition receptors on immune cells, amplifying the prothrombotic and pro-atherogenic milieu.
